Closing a gap between marketing and product, from workshop to shipped feature

Breakroom's TikTok content promised workers they could find out if they were underpaid, but the product didn't deliver on that promise yet. I ran a workshop, designed the prototype, and tested it with real users, and it even tested in a live advert in a rare moment of product and marketing working closely together.

The problem

Breakroom's TikTok marketing was built around a simple, compelling hook: "find out if you're underpaid." It was working — the videos were driving traffic. But there was a gap between the promise and the product.

What Breakroom actually showed was employer-level pay data: whether a company pays a living wage, whether it pays fairly relative to the sector. Useful, but not personal. A worker landing on the site after seeing that TikTok was looking for something that spoke directly to them — their pay, their situation. That individual signal didn't exist yet.

The results tour — the sequence of screens a worker sees immediately after completing the Breakroom Quiz — ended with your rating dial, the good things about your job, and the bad things. There was no moment that said: here's what your pay actually means.

Starting with a workshop

I kicked this project off with an in-person workshop, bringing in our PM, CTO, Head of Design, and one software engineer. I lead this small group in a Crazy 8s exercise to get a broad range of creative ideas out in the open and build a shared direction within the team before any design work started.

The sketches that the team created

The demographic pay finding was a good example of how the workshop brought up interesting, but out-of-scope thinking. Ideas came up on the topic of how your pay compares to people the same age as you, or the same gender. Enabling a user to 'find their tribe' and feel meaningful comparison to other workers like them. This is complicated, because Breakroom doesn't collect demographic data in the quiz. It's a topic that has come up before, but comes with potentially removing people's anonymity. An interesting thing to revisit at a later stage.

The design

Following the workshop, I started with the team's sketches to explore and reflect on the ideas that had come out of the session. This was a new concept, so I had to work through a few different ways to frame pay data in a way that felt personal and trustworthy to a worker.

My initial explorations tried to show all the different ways you could slice pay data — a 'Spotify Wrapped' style sequence of screens, each surfacing a different comparison: how your pay stacks up against people doing the same job at your employer, what different employers pay for your job title, and a more curiosity-driven one — what other frontline jobs near you pay. The idea was to put all of them in front of users and see what actually resonated.

The prototype also informed a parallel conversation with the engineering team about how to score on pay — specifically, at what thresholds we'd say someone is paid fairly. That's not a design decision, but the prototype made the question concrete enough that those conversations could happen before any back-end work began.

Testing it

The UserBrain sessions were structured deliberately to mirror the real user journey, testing the full sequence rather than the prototype in isolation. This mattered because an important part of success was whether the product felt like it was delivering on the promise the advert had set — if someone still didn't feel like they'd found out if they were underpaid, the design hadn't done its job.

Step Task
1 "To start, we'd like you to watch one of our adverts. Please watch this short video and then return to the survey questions."
2 Take the Breakroom Quiz and see the existing results tour as it existed in production
3 View the prototype of the revised results tour, including the new pay findings

Separately, before a line of code was written, the Figma prototype was in a live TikTok advert. The marketing team screen-recorded the clickable prototype and used it directly in their creative. We were testing from both ends of the spectrum at once — does the prototype resonate in a brand new advert, and does it actually answer the expectation set by the existing one?

Outcome

The pay tour shipped. It became the closing sequence of the Breakroom Quiz results tour — the moment where a worker gets a direct, personal read on their pay situation rather than a generalised view of their employer.

The prototype-to-advert path also established a pattern for how the marketing and design teams could work together — using high-fidelity Figma prototypes as marketing assets early in the process, before engineering investment is committed.

Reflection

The thing I'm most pleased with on this project is the sequencing. Starting with a workshop before touching Figma meant the direction had buy-in from engineering and product from day one — which matters in a small team where you can't afford to design something the CTO later decides isn't buildable.

The demographic pay idea is the one I'd want to pick back up. Surfacing it in the workshop was the right outcome — it was too big to tackle in scope — but "you're underpaid compared to other women in this role" is a genuinely powerful finding for a user who might not have framing for what they deserve. It would require adding demographic questions to the quiz, which has its own implications for completion rates and trust. That's a worthwhile conversation to have properly.

If I ran it again I'd push harder to get quantitative data baked into the success criteria from the start. The UserBrain sessions gave good qualitative signal, but having pre-defined metrics — completion rate of the pay tour, click-through from the call to action — would have given us a cleaner read on whether it was working.
