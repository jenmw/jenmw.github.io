Closing a gap between marketing and product

End to end ownership from workshop to shipped feature

Summary
  • Breakroom's TikTok ads were driving traffic with the hook "find out if you're underpaid", but the product didn't yet deliver on that promise, and users were noticing.
  • I facilitated a cross-functional workshop to ideate on how the product could better fit this user need, then led the design end-to-end, from sketches to a tested, high-fidelity prototype.
  • Collaborating with product-marketing was integral, so I also worked with the marketing team to run the prototype directly in live ad creatives.
  • The pay tour shipped as the closing sequence of The Breakroom Quiz, and cost-per-acquisition dropped 30% when prototype screens ran in live creatives.

Brief

Why this project existed
TikTok content was performing — but creating a gap between what the product promised and what it delivered. Users were commenting negatively on ads and emailing the helpdesk about the missing feature.
How we measured success
After taking the quiz, users should clearly answer "am I well paid or not?" — confirmed through UserBrain research. The prototype also needed to hold up in live ad creative.

My role

Lead designer from start to finish. I facilitated the kick-off workshop, owned all design work, ran the UserBrain research, and collaborated with the marketing team to test the prototype in live ads. The wider team included our PM, CTO, Head of Design, and a software engineer.

The problem

Breakroom's TikTok marketing was built around a simple, compelling hook: "find out if you're underpaid." It was working, and the videos were driving traffic. But there was a gap between the promise and the product. And users were noticing, commenting negatively to our ads, small numbers even emailing the helpdesk to complain that the feature didn't exist.

Users could browse Breakroom pay pages for employer-level pay data, find out whether a company pays a living wage for example. But a worker landing on the quiz after seeing that TikTok was looking for something that spoke directly to them and their pay. That tailored quiz signal didn't exist yet.

The results tour (the sequence of screens a worker sees immediately after completing the Breakroom Quiz) ended with your rating dial, the good things about your job, and the bad things. There was no moment that said: here's what your pay actually means.

How I approached it

I kicked this project off with an in-person workshop, bringing in our PM, CTO, Head of Design, and one software engineer. I lead this small group in a Crazy 8s exercise to get a broad range of creative ideas out in the open and build a shared direction within the team before any design work started.

The sketches that the team created

The demographic pay finding was a good example of how the workshop brought up interesting, but out-of-scope thinking. Ideas came up on the topic of how your pay compares to people the same age as you, or the same gender. Enabling a user to 'find their tribe' and feel meaningful comparison to other workers like them. This is complicated, because Breakroom doesn't collect demographic data in the quiz. It's a topic that has come up before, but comes with potentially removing people's anonymity. An interesting thing to revisit at a later stage.

The design

Following the workshop, I started with the team's sketches to explore and reflect on the ideas that had come out of the session. This was a new concept, so I had to work through a few different ways to frame pay data in a way that felt personal and trustworthy to a worker.

My initial explorations tried to show all the different ways you could slice pay data — a 'Spotify Wrapped' style sequence of screens, each surfacing a different comparison: how your pay stacks up against people doing the same job at your employer, what different employers pay for your job title, and a more curiosity-driven one — what other frontline jobs near you pay. The idea was to put all of them in front of users and see what actually resonated.

Testing

The UserBrain sessions were structured deliberately to mirror the real user journey, testing the full sequence rather than the prototype in isolation. This mattered because an important part of success was whether the product felt like it was delivering on the promise the advert had set — if someone still didn't feel like they'd found out if they were underpaid, the design hadn't done its job.

Step Task (Small sample, 5 users)
1 "To start, we'd like you to watch one of our adverts. What information do you think the quiz will tell you about your pay? What do you expect to see?"
2 Users take the Breakroom Quiz with existing results tour. Ask "How difficult or easy was it to tell if you are well paid or not?"
3 Users see prototype of the proposed design for end of quiz. Ask "How difficult or easy was it to tell if you are well paid or not?"
4 "Think of someone in your life that works in a frontline job or is paid by the hour. Who are they? Would you recommend Breakroom to them? If so, how would you describe it to them?" Do users mention pay comparison as a compelling reason to use Breakroom?

In parallel with the Userbrain study, the Figma prototype was also featured in ads that ran on TikTok and Instagram. The marketing team screen-recorded the clickable prototype and used it directly in their creative. We were testing from both ends of the spectrum at once — does the prototype resonate in a brand new advert, and does it actually answer the expectation set by the existing one?

Outcome

The pay tour shipped. It became the closing sequence of the Breakroom Quiz results tour — the moment where a worker gets a direct, personal read on their pay situation rather than having to go digging for more abstract pay findings elsewhere on the site.

The UserBrain sessions confirmed the design was doing its job. Users understood what the data was telling them and understood what it meant for them personally.

It's great comparison. It's very easy to understand the data that's being shown to me. It was very easy to understand if I'm well paid or not.

Sherida

This is a good page that shows information, I'm underpaid because it says I get paid twelve pounds, but others are paid fifteen.

Eniola

Trying a prototype in advert turned out to be a success too, with the ads performing well. Our average cost per acquisition dropped from £0.88 to £0.62 after adding the prototype screen recording — a 30% reduction. The clip showing the prototype screens stayed in multiple ads going forward. Anecdotally we saw less TikTok comments and helpdesk tickets on the subject of Breakroom not delivering on its promise to tell you "the truth about your pay."

The only thing that didn't come through very strongly in the UserBrain sessions was hearing that people would recommend Breakroom specifically because of its pay comparison feature. However, that final question was a nice-to-have and didn't strongly impact the success of the feature for me.

What I'd do differently

What I'm most proud of is the end-to-end ownership. I ran the workshop at the start to get alignment on what was feasible, and was still actively involved at the other end, working with the marketing team to make sure they could get the clips they needed. I even built an alternate prototype with dollar values for their US-facing ads.

The demographic data question that came up in the workshop is one I'd love to see revisited, but it's much bigger than this project. It has the potential to create really emotive findings, for example being able to surface that an employer pays their male employees significantly more than their female employees would be a powerful finding. But collecting demographic data has real implications for quiz completion rates, and whether workers trust that their responses are truly anonymous.

If I ran this project again I'd like to stay closer to how the marketing team were measuring the success of the ads. I handed things over and moved on at the end, but it would have been a great chance to extend my overlap into a part of the company I don't usually work alongside. I think of myself as 'T-shaped', and that's exactly the kind of opportunity to learn outside of your own role.
